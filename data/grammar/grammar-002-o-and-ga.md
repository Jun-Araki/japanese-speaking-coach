# What the verb acts on, and what exists or is needed

## を — the thing the verb acts on

- 手紙**を**書きます。 (I write a letter.)
- 日本語**を**勉強します。 (I study Japanese.)
- 荷物**を**持ちます。 (I carry the luggage.)

A verb that acts on something takes 「を」 even when the English verb takes no
preposition and so gives you nothing to copy. 「勉強する」 and 「持つ」 both act on
something, so both take 「を」, never 「が」.

## が — what exists, what is needed, what you can do

A small group of words describes a *state* rather than an action, and what they
describe takes 「が」:

- あります／います — 質問**が**あります。 (I have a question.)
- 要る／必要だ — 印鑑**が**要ります。／許可**が**必要です。
- できる、分かる、好きだ — 日本語**が**分かります。

These feel like objects in English ("I have a question", "I need a stamp"), so 「を」
is the natural guess and the wrong one.

## が also introduces news

「が」 marks the plain subject of something you are reporting as new information:

- 雨**が**降ってきました。 (It has started raining.)
- バス**が**来ました。 (The bus has come.)

That is a different job from 「は」, which introduces something already in view — see
grammar-003.

## Leaving the particle out

Dropping 「を」 stays natural even in polite speech: 「コーヒー、飲みますか。」「これ、見て
ください。」 are ordinary, not sloppy.

「が」 is not the same. Before 「あります」「要ります」「必要です」, it is what tells the
listener which thing you mean, and dropping it in careful speech to a manager reads
as clipped, memo-like Japanese rather than relaxed speech:

- ✗ 明日までに返事必要です。 → ✓ 明日までに返事**が**必要です。

How polite the sentence has to be in the first place is decided by who is listening
— see grammar-008.
