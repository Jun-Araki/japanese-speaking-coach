# は marks what the sentence is about

「は」 is not the subject marker. It announces what you are going to talk about, and
everything after it is the comment on that thing.

- この本**は**面白いです。 (This book — it's interesting.)
- 妹**は**学生です。 (My sister — she's a student.)

Compare grammar-002: 「が」 introduces something as news, 「は」 picks up something
already in view and says something about it.

## Questions start from the thing being asked about

English puts the question word first — "Where are you from?", "What is your job?"
Japanese puts the *thing* first and the question word last. Copying the English
order produces sentences that are understandable but not Japanese:

- ✗ どこがあなたの会社ですか。 → ✓ お勤め先**は**どちらですか。

「あなた」 is not what is wrong there. 「あなたは何時に来ますか。」 is a perfectly good
sentence. The defect is the word order, which starts from 「どこが」 instead of from
the thing being asked about.

The same shape catches 「を」. What you are asking about is the topic:

- ✗ あれ**を**いくらですか。 → ✓ あれ**は**いくらですか。

## Time words at the head of a sentence

A bare time word at the front is fine when it is only saying *when* — 「明日、会議が
あります。」 A time word takes 「は」 as soon as the sentence sets that day against the
others, which is most of the time in conversation:

- 今日**は**早く帰れます。 (Today — unlike usually — I can leave early.)
- 昨日**は**暑かったです。

Telling a manager what you can do *today* is that second kind: you are saying today
is different, so 「は」 belongs there.

## A phrase that moves keeps its particle

Japanese lets you push part of a sentence out behind the main clause when it comes
to you late. The particle goes with it:

- 暑いですね、今日**は**。
- もう読みましたか、あの本**は**。

Leaving the particle off here does not read as casual so much as unfinished — the
phrase arrives with nothing linking it back to the clause it belongs to.

For clock times, which take 「に」 and never 「は」, see grammar-001.
